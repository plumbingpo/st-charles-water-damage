Dealing With AC Leak Water Damage in St. Charles

Your air conditioner pulls moisture out of the air all day, and that water has to go somewhere. Normally it drips off the coil into a pan and leaves through a small drain line. When that line clogs with algae and dust, the pan fills and spills over. Attic and closet air handlers send the spill directly into the surrounding ceiling, walls or flooring.

Slow leaks do more damage than people expect. Drywall draws the water in, the platform beneath the unit gets saturated, and moisture slides under the floor, with mold often underway before any stain appears. Tracking the full extent of the moisture, drying it and making repairs is our part. You will still need an HVAC technician to clear the drain or fix the unit, and we can work around their visit.

Do This First

Your First Steps

  1. Turn Off the ACSet the thermostat to off, which stops the unit from producing more water. Flip the air handler's breaker too when water is close to its wiring.
  2. Keep Clear of Wet ElectricalCeiling lights, outlets and the unit itself are off limits while wet, and stay that way until their circuit has been turned off.
  3. Empty the Drain PanUse a wet-vac or rags to take the standing water out of the pan and stop the spill, provided the pan is safe to reach. Skip this step if it means crossing a soaked attic.
  4. Soak Up What You CanBlot wet carpet, wipe hard floors, set a bucket under any ceiling drip, and move boxes, clothes and furniture away from the closet or the stained area.
  5. Bring In Two ProsYou need two calls: one to us so we can measure the wet area, one to an HVAC technician who will unclog the drain. Keep the AC off until the repair is made.
Why It Happens

How It Usually Starts

  • Missing insulation on cold lines and ductwork, so condensation forms and falls.
  • Units that rely on a condensate pump, when that pump quits or gets unplugged.
  • The drain pipe for condensate blocked by a buildup of dust and algae.
  • More meltwater than the pan can handle after an iced-over coil thaws.
  • A dirty filter or low refrigerant, both of which make the coil ice up.
What Is at Risk

What the Water Damages

Insulation Around the Unit

Overflow in the attic sinks into the surrounding insulation, which keeps the drywall under it wet. Wet insulation loses its value and often has to be replaced.

Mold Near the Return

Damp drywall and wood beside the air handler can grow mold within 24 to 48 hours, and the return air can pull spores into the ductwork and through the house.

Ceilings Under the Unit

An attic air handler leaks onto the drywall beneath it. The ceiling stains first, then sags, and a section soaked for days can fall into the hallway or bedroom below.

Closet Walls and Platform

Many closet installations put the air handler on a wood platform, with the return box below it. Leaks decay the stand and climb the closet drywall, concealed by the equipment itself.

How We Fix It

What Happens After You Call

1

Measure the Spread

Stains show only part of it, so we scan the area by the air handler, the walls or ceiling beside it and the nearby flooring with a thermal camera and moisture meters.

2

Remove Water and Wet Drywall

Standing water in the pan, the platform and the carpet is extracted. Saturated or moldy insulation and drywall are removed, which opens the cavity to dry air.

3

Dry the Structure

Dehumidifiers and air movers stay on for a few days, usually 3 to 5, and we log the moisture readings. Access to the air handler is kept open for your HVAC technician.

4

Clean and Restore

Since condensate left standing for days is seldom clean, we apply an antimicrobial to the surfaces it touched. After that, our crew puts back the drywall, insulation, baseboard and floor and paints the repair to blend in.

Will Insurance Pay?

Does Insurance Cover It?

Coverage for an AC leak usually comes down to timing. A sudden pan overflow that you dealt with promptly is the kind of loss homeowners policies often cover. A drip the adjuster believes lasted weeks or months can be classed as gradual leakage or poor maintenance, and the claim denied. Either way, the cost of fixing the AC unit is typically left out. Our photos and readings are available for your claim, and your policy has the final word.

What It Costs

What Drives the Price

  • Rot in the stand beneath the air handler, which can mean rebuilding it.
  • Length of the leak, counted from the first drip to the day the system was stopped.
  • The water category, because Category 2 needs more cleaning than Category 1.
  • The area of drywall and floor covering that cannot be saved.
